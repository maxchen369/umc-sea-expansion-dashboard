## Context

The Singapore operation is a **branch** of a Taiwan head office: a permanent establishment with no separate legal personality. That one fact shapes everything else. Legally, head office and branch are the same company, yet Singapore still taxes the profit attributable to the branch and can apply withholding tax (WHT) to interest the branch bears.

## Framework 1 — Intercompany loan vs. head-office funding

| | Intercompany loan | Head-office funding (capital allotment) |
|---|---|---|
| Legal nature | A loan deemed to exist inside one legal person | An internal transfer of operating or capital funds |
| Interest | Required, at an arm's-length rate | None |
| Singapore withholding tax | Applies to interest borne by the branch (15%) | Not applicable, because no interest is paid |
| Singapore tax shield | Interest is deductible | None |
| Transfer pricing | Required (OECD Authorised Approach) | Not applicable |
| WHT exemption | Case-by-case ministerial exemption | Not needed |

**Decision rule.** A loan only creates value when the head office has matching interest expense of its own (for example, it funds itself with a straight bond) *and* the branch has enough taxable profit to use the deduction. When the head office funds with its own cash or a zero-coupon instrument, withholding tax and any home-country tax on the interest can outweigh the Singapore deduction. In that case direct funding is cheaper, simpler and carries less transfer-pricing risk.

**Red line: substance over form.** Direct funding holds only if the money genuinely stays and is used in Singapore. Funds that round-trip quickly, or paperwork that looks like a loan (agreements, interest rates, repayment schedules), can be recharacterized as lending and brought back into withholding tax.

## Framework 2 — Five funding routes

| Route | Instrument | Interest deductible | Cross-border WHT | EPS impact | Main constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 · Head office borrows and on-lends to the branch | Debt | Yes | Yes, unless exempted | None | Exemption outcome; arm's-length margin |
| 2 · Head office injects equity | Equity | No | None | Dilutive | No tax shield; funds are hard to return |
| 3 · Branch borrows from local banks | Debt | Yes | None | None | Relies on head-office credit support |
| 4 · Head office borrows for its own needs and funds the branch from own cash | Debt + own funds | Yes | None | None | Needs genuine, independent needs on both sides |
| 5 · Head office issues a zero-coupon convertible bond | Convertible | Almost none | None | Dilutive on conversion | Minimal tax shield; value is the lowest cash cost |

What the comparison showed:

1. **Debt beats equity only after withholding-tax leakage is counted.** The interest deduction is real, but cross-border interest can be eroded by withholding tax.
2. **Who receives the interest decides whether withholding tax applies.** Interest paid to the head office can trigger it; interest paid to a local bank does not.
3. **Compare funding costs on matched tenors.** Pairing an overnight rate in one market with a one-month rate in the other pointed to the wrong route; on matched one-month tenors the ranking reversed.
4. **The real decision is total funding cost × risk, not the tax shield alone.** On a cost-versus-risk map the local bank loan is the most robust, the convertible has the lowest cash cost at the price of future dilution, route 4 is cheapest on paper but carries the highest anti-avoidance risk unless the substance is real, and an equity injection is usually the last resort.
5. **The intercompany margin cuts both ways.** A higher margin increases the branch's deduction but also the withholding-tax base and the head office's taxable income. It has to sit in a range both tax authorities would accept as arm's length.

## Framework 3 — Withholding-tax operating checklist

A reusable checklist for any future "head office raises funds, branch uses them" arrangement:

- [ ] **Classify the funds first.** Is this a loan used in branch operations, with interest borne by the branch? Or is it head-office cash that is only held in Singapore and unrelated to branch operations?
- [ ] **Segregate from day one.** Use separate bank accounts and ledgers for borrowed funds and own funds. Traceability is the main defense against misclassification.
- [ ] **Document the loan.** Agreement, purpose, and an arm's-length rate (funding cost plus a margin supported by a functional and risk analysis).
- [ ] **Confirm the WHT treatment before the first interest entry.** Crediting interest to an account, not only paying cash, can trigger the obligation.
- [ ] **Decide the exemption strategy up front.** Ministerial exemptions are case-by-case and conditional, and they are credible only when funds are genuinely deployed in Singapore operations. Parking funds briefly to shrink the tax base works against an exemption and against economic-substance expectations.
- [ ] **File on time.** Singapore withholding tax is generally due by the 15th of the second month after the date of payment.
- [ ] **Keep both sides consistent.** The branch's Singapore filing and the head office's home-country filing should tell the same story.
